7 — INFLUENCER MONEY BIBLE

How the creator economy really makes money, including the hidden money. Reverse-engineered for Terry Bryant, Newswire Hollywood and PR STARPOWER. Edition 1, November 5, 2026. Written as ebook chapters.

How to read this: every fact carries a source number [S#] that matches the Sources list at the end. Anything marked ESTIMATE is our estimate or an industry range, not an audited figure. Anything marked VERIFY comes from background knowledge or a single weak source and must be checked before it goes in a published ebook.

Chapter 1 — The Size of the Game

  • Goldman Sachs Research projects the creator economy reaches about $480 billion by 2027, roughly double its ~$250 billion size [S1].
  • The money is concentrated: Goldman says only about 4% of creators worldwide earn more than $100,000 a year [S1]. Lesson: the money is not in being a creator. It is in being the supplier to creators: management, access, deals, data and distribution. That is our lane.
  • The two engines Goldman names: brand (influencer) marketing and platform payouts tied to short-video ads [S1].
  • Meta says it paid creators nearly $3 billion in 2025 through its monetization programs [S2].
  • Forbes' 2025 Top Creators: the top 50 earned an estimated $853 million combined, up 18% on the prior year [S3].

Chapter 2 — The Ten Experts Worth Studying

Chosen for proven results: people who built businesses that pay, not commentators. Study their methods, then copy the structure, not the personality.

  1. Reed Duchscher — founder, Night (talent management). Built Night in 2015 off Dude Perfect, then managed MrBeast to the top; runs management, brand partnerships and a venture fund side by side; Peter Chernin invested in both the management firm and fund; Night bought Bottle Rocket Management in Nov 2024 [S4][S5]. MrBeast moved his primary management in-house in 2024, while Duchscher stayed on Feastables [S6]. Method: manage the creator like a media company; take equity in what you help build. Teaches: own a piece, not just a percentage.
  2. Ryan Detert — founder, Influential (acquired by Publicis Groupe, 2024). Built an AI-matched creator marketplace; reported to manage about 3.5 million creators and work with 60% of the Fortune 500 [S7] (VERIFY figures with Publicis). Method: data and scale win agency budgets. Teaches: the big ad holding companies will buy creator networks; build one worth buying.
  3. Joe Gagliese — co-founder and CEO, Viral Nation. Raised $198 million at a ~$650 million valuation; profitable since its 2014 founding; launched direct-to-consumer cosmetics brands, one reportedly at $20 million annual revenue [S8]. Method: agency + talent + owned brands. Teaches: use creator reach to sell your own products, not only other people's.
  4. Neil Waller — co-founder and CEO, Whalar (VERIFY title). Whalar's FY2024 revenue is reported at about $109.8 million, gross profit ~$39.2 million [S9]; 2025 round valued it around $400 million, with Shopify Ventures participating [S10]. Method: brands pay Whalar for campaigns; Whalar pays creators; the gap (about 36% gross margin, our calculation from [S9]) is the business. Teaches: the margin is in packaging creators for brands.
  5. Li Jin — venture investor, coined "passion economy." Argues the industry must build a creator "middle class," not only superstars; taught a free creator-economy course [S11]. Teaches: the 10K–250K creator is the underserved market. That is where our first 10 signings come from.
  6. Jim Louderback — former VidCon CEO; editor of "Inside the Creator Economy." Newsletter reported at 32,000+ readers [S12]. Teaches: diversification and creator-led media companies. A model for our interview series.
  7. Brendan Gahan — Creator Authority; former chief social officer at Mekanism. Early architect of YouTube creator marketing [S7][S13]. Teaches: brands buy outcomes; package creators as performance media.
  8. Alex Cooper — founder, Unwell; "Call Her Daddy." A creator who became the platform: SiriusXM deal reported at up to $125 million over three years, including global ad-sales rights to her whole Unwell network [S14][S15]. Teaches: own the network, then sell the distribution rights.
  9. Jimmy Donaldson (MrBeast) — Beast Industries. Feastables reported at about $250 million in 2024 sales and $20 million+ profit, while the media arm lost money [S16][S17]. Teaches: content is the marketing; the product is the money.
  10. Goldman Sachs Research (Eric Sheridan's internet team — VERIFY author credit). The market-sizing reference everyone cites [S1]. Teaches: pitch investors and sponsors with institutional numbers, not vibes.

The common posting and filming playbook (pattern across the above): one hero format repeated with discipline; a hook in the first 1–3 seconds; daily short-form clipped from long-form; a live or event moment that creates "you had to be there" urgency; a consistent upload cadence; and a product or community to send the audience to.

Chapter 4 — Brand Deals and the Deliverables Table

How it works, step by step: (1) the brand or its agency sends a brief; (2) creator or manager sends a rate card and media kit (audience, reach, engagement, past results); (3) negotiate scope: deliverables, usage rights, exclusivity, timing, approvals; (4) contract; (5) content made, brand approves; (6) post with FTC disclosure; (7) send recap report; (8) invoice, often 50% upfront and 50% on delivery (ESTIMATE common practice).

Typical rates per deliverable (industry ranges, ESTIMATE; vendor sources disagree) [S27][S28]:

  • Instagram feed post: nano ~$50–$300; macro ~$2,500–$50,000+.
  • TikTok video: nano ~$25–$125; macro ~$1,250–$100,000+ (wide disagreement).
  • YouTube integration/video: nano ~$100–$300; macro up to ~$20,000+; Shorts priced lower.
  • Instagram Story: commonly priced at a fraction of a feed post (ESTIMATE 25%–50%).
  • Live stream mention or hosted live: priced per hour, often equal to or above a feed post (ESTIMATE).

The add-ons, where the real money is [S29]: paid usage rights +30% to +100% of base depending on term; whitelisting/Spark Ads 30%–100% of base per month; full buyout +100%–150%; raw footage +30%–50%; rush +25%–50%; exclusivity priced as a separate fee (no reliable standard; ESTIMATE +25%–100% per category per month of lockout).

Rule of thumb used by managers (ESTIMATE): base fee ≈ $10–$25 per 1,000 followers on Instagram for engaged accounts, adjusted for engagement and niche. Never quote only by follower count; quote by results.

Chapter 5 — Platform Payouts

YouTube

  • Partner Program: creators get 55% of long-form ad revenue and 45% of their allocated share of the Shorts ad pool [S30].
  • Thresholds commonly reported: 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 watch-hours in 12 months, or 10 million Shorts views in 90 days [S30] (VERIFY current numbers on YouTube Help).
  • RPM (what you keep per 1,000 views) is not published by YouTube; third-party estimates: entertainment ~$1–$5, finance ~$8–$30; US audiences pay far more than India or Brazil [S31]. Lesson: entertainment news needs volume plus sponsorships; ads alone are thin.
  • Other YouTube money: channel memberships, Super Chat, Super Thanks, YouTube Shopping affiliate.

Facebook and Instagram

  • Facebook Content Monetization merges in-stream ads, Reels ads and performance bonuses into one program covering Reels, long video, Stories, photos and text posts; it is invite-only with an interest form in the Professional Dashboard [S2][S32].
  • Creator Fast Track (2026): creators with 1M+ followers on other platforms can get $3,000/month guaranteed for three months [S2][S32].
  • Instagram: Subscriptions, gifts on Reels, and brand-paid Partnership Ads. Bonus programs come and go by invitation (VERIFY current status).

TikTok

  • Creator Rewards Program: 18+, 10,000 followers, 100,000 views in the last 30 days; only videos over one minute count; paid on qualified views weighted by engagement, watch time, originality and audience location [S33]. Rates are not official; third-party estimates around $0.40–$1.00 per 1,000 qualified views (ESTIMATE).
  • LIVE gifts: viewers buy Coins and send gifts, which convert to Diamonds the creator cashes out. TikTok keeps a large share (widely reported at about 50% — VERIFY against TikTok's Virtual Items Policy).
  • Subscriptions: monthly paid fan subscriptions for eligible creators.

Twitch and Kick

  • Twitch: subscriptions, Bits, ads; standard 50/50 sub split, up to 70/30 through Partner Plus (VERIFY).
  • Kick: widely reported 95/5 subscription split in creators' favor (VERIFY), plus large signing deals (xQc ~$70M–$100M) [S20].

Going-live and appearance fees

Paid appearances (club hosting, store openings, brand activations, event livestreams) are priced per hour plus travel. Top creators command five and six figures (ESTIMATE). Always add: content rights, a recap post, and a capped number of photo ops.

Chapter 6 — Affiliate and Shopping Money

  • Amazon Associates / Amazon Influencer Program: commissions set by category, from about 1% to 20%; Influencer Program rates match Associates rates; Amazon cuts rates over time [S34][S35]. Storefront = a creator shop page on Amazon, plus shoppable videos on product pages (onsite commissions).
  • LTK: retailer-negotiated commissions, commonly 5%–15%+; app shows per-product rates [S36].
  • ShopMy: reported 5%–25%, popular with beauty and fashion; brands also pay gifting and paid collabs through it [S37].
  • TikTok Shop: reported US average ~13%, open-plan rates from ~5% (electronics) to 30% (beauty) [S38]; brands also pay flat fees for videos and lives.
  • The tail: affiliate content keeps earning for years. An evergreen gift guide or "best of" list is an annuity. That is why Doc 8 starts gift guides now.

Chapter 7 — UGC, Licensing and Content Rights

  • UGC (content made for a brand to post or advertise with, not posted by the creator): commonly $75–$300 per video for beginners, $300–$1,000 mid-tier, $600–$3,000+ top tier [S29].
  • Licensing: the same video sold again for paid ads, other markets, or other platforms. Charge by term, territory and media.
  • Likeness licensing is the new frontier: Khaby Lame's deal includes commercial rights to his brand and an AI twin [S18]. Every creator contract we write must say who owns the creator's likeness, voice and AI replicas: the creator does, licensed for a set term only.

Chapter 8 — Products, Communities, Courses, Events, Publishing

  • Product lines: the biggest money but the biggest risk. Feastables ~$250M sales in 2024 [S16]; Prime Hydration's UK revenue fell roughly 70% in 2024 [S39] — hype fades, distribution and repeat purchase win.
  • Paid communities and subscriptions: Patreon, YouTube memberships, Instagram and TikTok subscriptions, Discord, Skool. Recurring revenue is valued higher by buyers.
  • Courses and coaching: high margin; reputational risk if overpromised. FTC rules on earnings claims apply.
  • Events, meet-and-greets, gifting lounges: tickets + sponsors + content. La Velada shows the ceiling [S22].
  • Publishing and newsletters: beehiiv's Ad Network pays per open or click, says it takes no cut, and reports $37M+ paid to creators [S40][S41]; books, audio and podcast deals (Call Her Daddy at up to $125M [S14]).

Chapter 9 — The Hidden Money (Forever Money)

This is what nobody puts in the YouTube videos about "how I make money."

  1. Equity instead of (or on top of) fees. Alix Earle's stake in Poppi before its $1.95B sale to PepsiCo [S26]. Ask every early-stage brand for equity or warrants in exchange for promotion.
  2. Selling the company, not the content. Khaby Lame's $900M+ stake sale [S18]; MrBeast raising at ~$5B [S17].
  3. Distribution deals. Platforms and networks pay upfront for exclusivity and ad-sales rights (SiriusXM–Cooper [S14]; Kick–xQc [S20]).
  4. Back catalog. Old videos keep earning ad revenue, can be licensed to streaming and TV, dubbed into new languages, and clipped for Shorts. A catalog is an asset buyers pay multiples for (VERIFY specific catalog sale comps before publication).
  5. Affiliate tails. Links in old content earn every holiday season.
  6. Royalties. Product lines on a licensing model pay a percentage of every sale without inventory risk (ESTIMATE typical consumer-product royalty 5%–15% of wholesale).
  7. Residuals and rights reversion. Content licensed for a term comes back to you and can be sold again.
  8. Data and audience ownership. An email list and a phone number list survive any algorithm change. That is why the newsletter is in Doc 8.
  9. Management upside. Managers who take equity in client businesses (Night's venture fund model [S4]) participate in the exit.

Chapter 10 — How Agencies and Managers Make Money

  • Commission: managers commonly 15%–20%; agents and agencies 10%–20%; combined can reach 25%–40% of a creator's deal [S42]. CAA, UTA and WME digital divisions reported at 10%–25% for creators with 100K+ followers (single vendor source; VERIFY) [S42].
  • California law: the Talent Agencies Act requires a license to procure work in California; managers who book deals without a license risk losing commissions (VERIFY scope with counsel before PR STARPOWER books deals in California) [S42].
  • Campaign margin (brand side): the agency sells the campaign to the brand, pays the creators, keeps the spread (Whalar ~36% gross margin by our calculation) [S9].
  • Retainers: brands pay monthly for always-on creator programs; creators sometimes pay retainers for PR (we will not charge creators retainers for the first 10).
  • Packaging: bundling several clients into one deal (a show, a tour, a lounge) and charging a packaging fee.
  • Owned brands and media: Viral Nation's cosmetics lines [S8]; Night's venture fund [S4].
  • Exit: Publicis bought Influential [S7]; Night bought Bottle Rocket [S5]. Build to be bought or to buy.

Chapter 12 — The ROI Playbook: Never Leave a Deal Empty-Handed

The rule: whenever we give value (product placement, an appearance, coverage access, an influencer lounge, introductions), we ask for one or more measurable returns, in this order:

  1. Fee (cash first).
  2. Content rights (we can use the photos and video).
  3. Affiliate or discount code (we earn on every sale).
  4. Recap data (reach, posts, redemptions) — fuels our next sales pitch.
  5. Introductions (their partnerships lead, their agency, their next event).
  6. Case study rights (we can publish the results).

Chapter 13 — Business Plan: PR STARPOWER Influencer Management and Agency Division

Services

  • Talent management: brand deals, rate cards, negotiation, contracts, invoicing and collections.
  • Access: red carpets, premieres, influencer lounges, award-season events (our Events Calendar is the edge).
  • Publicity: press, Newswire Hollywood features (disclosed if the client is ours), media training.
  • Brand side: we package our roster for brands as campaigns and lounges.

Pricing

  • Creators: 15%–20% commission on deals we source or negotiate; no retainer for the first 10 signings (market norms [S42]).
  • Brands: campaign fee = creator costs + 25%–35% margin (ESTIMATE, consistent with agency margins [S9]); lounge packages $2,500–$10,000+ (ESTIMATE).
  • Equity: ask for equity or royalties in any client product we help launch.

Contracts (must-have clauses)

  • Management agreement: term (12 months, renewable), commission rate, post-term commission on deals we closed (sunset, e.g., 6–12 months), creator owns their likeness and AI replicas, FTC compliance, termination for cause, no procurement of employment in California without proper licensing (VERIFY with counsel).
  • Brand agreement: deliverables table, usage rights term and media, whitelisting, exclusivity fees, approvals, payment terms, kill fee, morality clause both ways.

How to sign the first 10

  1. Source: Nov 7 lounge list, our red-carpet contacts, and micro-creators who already tag Newswire Hollywood.
  2. Filter: 10K–250K followers, engagement above niche average, clean brand-safety history, LA-based or LA-active.
  3. Offer: one paid deal in the first 60 days or they can walk, no fee.
  4. Prove it: book each signing one lounge or event with a paid or traded return inside 30 days.
  5. Showcase: feature the roster in "The Money Behind the Fame" (disclosed).

Global recruitment

  • Offer international creators "the Hollywood experience": awards-season trips with lounges, red carpets and interviews, paid by sponsors.
