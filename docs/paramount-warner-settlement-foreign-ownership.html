Paramount settles with 12 state attorneys general to save its $111 billion Warner Bros. Discovery takeover

The deal survives a lawsuit that could have killed it — at the cost of a film-output floor, a promise to run the two studios separately, and an FCC ruling that clears the merged company to be 49.5% foreign-owned.

By the Deals Desk, Newswire Hollywood  |  Sept 22, 2026  |  Los Angeles

Paramount's owners have made a series of concessions to a coalition of 12 Democratic state attorneys general, resolving a lawsuit that had put the company's $111 billion takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery in real jeopardy.

The settlement still has to be approved by a judge. If it is, Paramount will be bound to operate Warner Bros. Studios and Paramount Pictures as separate businesses — for now — and to a hard floor on how much it makes. Together the two studios must release at least 30 films a year for the next two years, rising to 32 a year for the three years after that, or face a financial penalty.

That output floor is the part of this worth watching. The standard fear when two major studios combine is not that prices go up; it is that the slate goes down, and the number of films that get made and the number of people employed making them quietly contracts. The attorneys general appear to have negotiated directly against that outcome rather than against the merger itself.

California Attorney General Rob Bonta was explicit that settling is not the same as blessing the deal. The agreement, he said, was not a vote of support for the merger but "a strong solution that protects competition and consumers."

The settlement lands days after a separate and arguably larger decision. On September 18 the Federal Communications Commission approved an enormous infusion of foreign capital to finance the transaction. The merged company would be 49.5% owned by foreign interests, among them the sovereign wealth funds of Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Abu Dhabi.

The FCC went further than the deal as structured requires, ruling that foreign ownership of the company could run as high as 100%. Paramount has said those stakes will be silent, non-voting shares.

"Silent and non-voting" is a meaningful legal distinction and a thinner practical one. A shareholder with half the capital and no vote still has the standing that comes with being the reason the lights are on. Whether that shapes what a combined Paramount-Warner greenlights, and what it declines to greenlight, is not a question a merger condition can answer in advance.

Newswire Hollywood is following the judicial approval and will report the ruling when it lands.
