Freda Payne stopped time at Catalina: a sold-out night with the legendary ladies of song

Nine women, one voice, and a room that would not let her go. Freda Payne's tribute to the ladies of jazz played to a full house on Sunset on VMAs weekend — and she knew half of the women she was singing about.

By the Music Desk, Newswire Hollywood  |  Sept 29, 2026  |  Catalina Jazz Club, Los Angeles

There is a trick the very best singers do, and almost nobody can do it on purpose. They stop the clock. Not slow it — stop it. Saturday night on Sunset Boulevard, in a room that had sold out before the doors opened, Freda Payne did it for ninety minutes and made it look like nothing at all.

Freda Payne Portrays and Honors Legendary Ladies of Song is a tribute show, which is a phrase that usually means a karaoke night with better lighting. This was not that. This was a woman standing inside her own history, singing about people she had actually stood next to, and the difference is the entire show.

She was not covering these women. She knew them.

The idea came from Donald B. Welch, the playwright, director and producer who wrote and directed the evening and presented it under the Don B. Welch banner. Payne and Welch have been friends for years, and it was Welch who put it to her.

“He said, Freda, I want you to do this show honoring several ladies — Ella Fitzgerald, who was one of my favorite, favorite ladies of jazz. Gloria Lynne. Dakota Staton and Sarah Vaughan, Lena Horne, Nancy Wilson. All these ladies were actually some of my favorite singers back when I started wanting to be a professional singer. Peggy Lee. Dinah Washington.”Freda Payne to Newswire Hollywood

That list is the show. Nine women, and Payne moved between them without ever disappearing into impersonation. She sang “How High the Moon” first — Ella's, by way of Ella in Berlin — and the room understood immediately what kind of night it was going to be. Payne is careful to note the song is a standard. Ella's version is the one that got carved in stone.

The songs

  • “How High the Moon”Ella Fitzgerald
  • “I Wish You Love”Gloria Lynne
  • “The Late Late Show”Dakota Staton
  • “Body and Soul”Sarah Vaughan
  • “Guess Who I Saw Today”Nancy Wilson

“I Wish You Love” was one of Gloria Lynne's big hits, though Payne's own favourite of hers is a deeper cut — “Love, I Found You,” early seventies, and she says the title like it still belongs to her.

The Dakota Staton number is where the evening cracked open. “The Late Late Show” sent Payne back sixty-odd years in about four bars, and afterwards she could still walk you there:

“I can picture myself now, on vacation up in a summer resort back in Michigan, because I was still living in Detroit, and I was like sixteen years old. It was a Black summer resort at the time in Michigan. It was called Idlewild. We had a cottage up there. My sister and I were allowed to go to matinees because we couldn't go to the nightclubs at night. We were still in our teens. And I remember her song, ‘The Late Late Show.’”Freda Payne to Newswire Hollywood

You cannot rehearse that. A sixteen-year-old in Idlewild, allowed into the matinee and not the midnight show, hearing Dakota Staton and deciding something about her own life — that girl was on the Catalina stage on Saturday, inside a woman who has been a professional for six decades.

The ones she knew

Sarah Vaughan, Payne met and came to know. She described the voice the way a musician would, not a fan:

“Her voice was like listening to a violin or a viola. Very soothing. She was a great jazz singer.”Freda Payne on Sarah Vaughan

She sang “Body and Soul” for Sarah, with the caveat that Sarah did everything.

Lena Horne got the sharpest read of the night. Known for her beauty, Payne said, and her class, and her style — and then, without a pause: “She wasn't a pussycat kind of a woman. She was like, don't mess with me, buddy.” Tough. And out of all of them, the most beautiful.

Nancy Wilson got “Guess Who I Saw Today,” which is the correct and only choice, and Payne knows exactly why: “The moment you hear her voice, you know it's Nancy Wilson.”

And then Dinah Washington, who Payne met young and was frightened of.

“Dinah Washington was a sister. She didn't take no mess from nobody. She would get in your face. She would fight men. When I first met her, I was scared, because I didn't think she liked me — I was young and cute back then. But for some reason, she liked me. I think it's because I kind of stood up to her. She could tell I was no pushover.”Freda Payne on Dinah Washington

Washington died at thirty-nine. Payne says it plainly and moves on, which is somehow worse, and better, than dwelling on it.

This is what a tribute is supposed to be and almost never is: testimony.

The company

Thomasina Gross opened the show and stole a considerable piece of it as Eartha Kitt. Payne's description is the one to use, because it is exactly right:

“When you see her perform — first of all, just to see her physically, she looks like Eartha Kitt. Her body is like Eartha Kitt. And she does this portrayal like spot on.”Freda Payne on Thomasina Gross

The evening was announced by Gregory Abbott Jr. — Payne's son — who introduced Gross, introduced his mother, and held the room between numbers with the ease of somebody who has been around this his whole life. Payne made a point of it twice.

Actors Terryl DaLuz and Kyrel Jaundoo filled out the staging. Chuka Aruh and Mi-cha-El Wat escorted.

The band did not coast for a second. Josh Nelson was musical director from the piano, and Payne's verdict on him was unqualified: there are a lot of good pianists, and he is one of the best. Chris Colangelo on bass. Ricky Woodard on saxophone — the Ricky Woodard, as Payne put it, and anyone who has spent a night in an LA jazz room knows why the definite article is doing work there. Marcello Carelli on drums.

The room

Loretta Devine was in. So was Stefanie Powers. They were not the only ones. On the same weekend the VMAs took over downtown and every camera in the city pointed at the Peacock Theater, the hottest ticket in Los Angeles was a sold-out jazz room on Sunset holding six hundred people's worth of attention with a standard from 1940.

That is not nostalgia. That is a market telling you something.

What a birthday looks like

Payne turned a year older on September 19, one week before the show. She said from the stage that she was blessed to see another year and still be working, and thanked God for it, and noted that a lot of the women being honoured that evening did not get to.

Dinah Washington was thirty-nine. That is the whole sentence. Freda Payne opened her new birthday year with a sold-out house on Sunset Boulevard, singing her heroes back into the room, with her son calling her name from the wings.

Show-stopping does not cover it. She set the town on fire.

Next

Payne sails at the end of October aboard Holland America, performing on a cruise out of Fort Lauderdale to Puerto Rico and back, with Darlene Love and Wayne Newton also on the bill.
