Freda Payne set Sunset Boulevard on fire: the sold-out night she sang her heroes back into the room

Nine legends, one voice, a gold lamé flare and a “Fever” that stopped the clock. On VMAs weekend, with the whole industry looking downtown, the hottest ticket in Los Angeles was a jazz room on Sunset.

By the Music Desk, Newswire Hollywood  |  Photographs by Terry Bryant  |  Sept 30, 2026  |  Catalina Jazz Club, Los Angeles

Gold lamé, head to floor. Red light coming down like heat. A bass player behind her in a sequined gold jacket, a drummer holding the room at a whisper, and six hundred people on Sunset Boulevard who had stopped checking their phones.

Freda Payne walked into Freda Payne Portrays and Honors Legendary Ladies of Song on Saturday night carrying sixty years of professional life, and for ninety minutes she did the thing almost nobody can do on purpose. She stopped the clock.

This was not a tribute show. This was testimony.

The difference matters, and it is the whole review. A tribute show is a singer doing impressions of the dead. What happened at Catalina was a woman singing about people she had stood next to — who had frightened her, taught her, been kind to her when she was young and cute and nobody yet. Ella Fitzgerald. Sarah Vaughan. Dinah Washington. Lena Horne. Nancy Wilson. Peggy Lee. Gloria Lynne. Dakota Staton. Eartha Kitt.

Nine women. She knew a good many of them. You could hear it.

Donald B. Welch built the room she needed

Credit where the evening starts. Donald B. Welch — playwright, director, producer — wrote and directed this, presented it under the Don B. Welch banner, and had the idea in the first place. He and Payne have been friends for years, and it was Welch who pushed.

“He said, Freda, I want you to do this show honouring several ladies — Ella Fitzgerald, who was one of my favorite, favorite ladies of jazz. Gloria Lynne. Dakota Staton and Sarah Vaughan, Lena Horne, Nancy Wilson. All these ladies were some of my favorite singers back when I started wanting to be a professional singer. Peggy Lee. Dinah Washington.”Freda Payne to Newswire Hollywood

What Welch understood, and what most producers of this kind of show never work out, is that the star's memory is the material. He did not build a revue and hire Freda Payne to front it. He built a frame around what she already carried and then got out of the way. That is direction, and it is much harder than it looks.

The Fever

And then, on her birthday weekend, in gold, she did “Fever.”

Peggy Lee's song. Payne brought two men up onto the stage and worked the number between them — playing it, stretching it, taking her time. The room came apart.

Six decades in, on her birthday weekend, she did “Fever” with two men on stage and made it look like the easiest thing she has ever done.

Call that what it is. That is not an artist paying respects to a legend. That is an artist reminding a room that she is one.

The songs, and where they came from

She opened with “How High the Moon” — Ella's, by way of Ella in Berlin — and Payne is scrupulous about the credit: the song is a standard, but Ella's reading is the one carved in stone.

  • “How High the Moon”Ella Fitzgerald
  • “I Wish You Love”Gloria Lynne
  • “The Late Late Show”Dakota Staton
  • “Body and Soul”Sarah Vaughan
  • “Guess Who I Saw Today”Nancy Wilson
  • “Fever”Peggy Lee

The Dakota Staton number is where the evening cracked open. “The Late Late Show” sent Payne back sixty-odd years in four bars, and afterwards she could still walk you there:

“I can picture myself now, on vacation up in a summer resort back in Michigan, because I was still living in Detroit, and I was like sixteen years old. It was a Black summer resort at the time in Michigan. It was called Idlewild. We had a cottage up there. My sister and I were allowed to go to matinees because we couldn't go to the nightclubs at night. We were still in our teens. And I remember her song, ‘The Late Late Show.’”Freda Payne to Newswire Hollywood

You cannot rehearse that. A sixteen-year-old at Idlewild, allowed into the matinee and not the midnight show, hearing Dakota Staton and deciding something permanent about her own life. That girl was on the Catalina stage on Saturday, inside a woman in gold lamé.

The ones she knew

Sarah Vaughan she met, and came to know. She describes the voice the way a musician would, not a fan:

“Her voice was like listening to a violin or a viola. Very soothing. She was a great jazz singer.”Freda Payne on Sarah Vaughan

She sang “Body and Soul” for Sarah, with the caveat that Sarah did everything.

Lena Horne got the sharpest read of the night. Known for her beauty, Payne said, her class, her style — and then, without a breath: “She wasn't a pussycat kind of a woman. She was like, don't mess with me, buddy.” Tough. And of all of them, the most beautiful.

Nancy Wilson got “Guess Who I Saw Today,” the only possible choice, and Payne knows exactly why: “The moment you hear her voice, you know it's Nancy Wilson.”

Then Dinah Washington, who Payne met young and was frightened of.

“Dinah Washington was a sister. She didn't take no mess from nobody. She would get in your face. She would fight men. When I first met her, I was scared, because I didn't think she liked me — I was young and cute back then. But for some reason, she liked me. I think it's because I kind of stood up to her. She could tell I was no pushover.”Freda Payne on Dinah Washington

Washington died at thirty-nine. Payne says it flatly and moves on, which lands harder than dwelling on it ever could.

Thomasina Gross does not do an impression of Eartha Kitt

Thomasina Gross opened the show and walked off with a considerable piece of it. Payne's own description is the one to use, because watching it, it is simply accurate:

“When you see her perform — first of all, just to see her physically, she looks like Eartha Kitt. Her body is like Eartha Kitt. And she does this portrayal like spot on.”Freda Payne on Thomasina Gross

Spot on is right, and it is the hardest note in the show to hit. Eartha Kitt is the most imitated woman in American entertainment and almost every imitation is a cartoon — the purr, the vowels, the joke. Gross plays the spine: the poise, the cool, the danger underneath. She does not send Eartha Kitt up. She stands her back up. On a bill this stacked, she holds her own, and that is the review.

Gregory Abbott Jr. hosted, and knew exactly what the night was

Gregory Abbott Jr. — Payne's son — hosted and announced. He introduced Thomasina Gross, he introduced his mother, and he carried the room between numbers with the ease of a man who has been standing in the wings of rooms like this his entire life.

Payne made a point of it twice in conversation afterwards, unprompted, the second time cutting herself off mid-sentence to make sure he got his due. Anyone who has ever watched a parent watch their child work will know what that sounded like.

The band was not decoration

They came out in gold too, and they earned it.

Josh Nelson was musical director from the piano, and Payne's verdict is unqualified — there are a lot of good pianists, and he is one of the best. It shows in the arrangements, which never once let the evening slide into lounge. Chris Colangelo on bass, holding the low end like a man with nothing to prove. Ricky Woodard on saxophone — the Ricky Woodard, as Payne put it, and anybody who has spent a night in a Los Angeles jazz room knows the definite article is doing real work there. Marcello Carelli on drums, playing the room rather than the kit.

Actors Terryl DaLuz and Kyrel Jaundoo filled out the staging. Chuka Aruh and Mi-cha-El Wat escorted.

The room, and the weekend

Loretta Devine was in. Stefanie Powers was in. They were not the only ones.

Consider the timing. This was VMAs weekend. Every camera in Los Angeles was pointed downtown at the Peacock Theater. Every publicist in town was working a carpet. And the hottest ticket in the city was a sold-out jazz room on Sunset Boulevard, where a woman in gold held six hundred people's undivided attention with a standard written in 1940.

That is not nostalgia. That is the market telling you something.

What a birthday looks like

Payne turned a year older on September 19, one week before the show. From the stage she said she was blessed to see another year and still be working, thanked God for it, and noted that a lot of the women being honoured that night never got to.

Dinah Washington was thirty-nine. That is the whole sentence.

Freda Payne opened her new birthday year with a sold-out house on Sunset Boulevard, in gold, singing her heroes back into the room, with her son calling her name.

She set the town on fire. There is no other way to file it.

Next

Payne sails at the end of October aboard Holland America, performing on a cruise out of Fort Lauderdale to Puerto Rico and back, on a bill that also carries Darlene Love and Wayne Newton.

If Legendary Ladies of Song comes back around — and on this evidence it should, and it should tour — do not wait for the review. It sold out before the doors opened this time.
